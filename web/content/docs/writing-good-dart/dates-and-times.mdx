## 30.1 An instant is not a day

A `DateTime` is a point on a timeline. It answers *when*, in the sense a stopwatch means, and
a calendar date is a different kind of thing: the 9th of September is the 9th of September in
Tashkent and in London, and it is not a moment at all — it is a box that about twenty-four
hours' worth of moments fall into, and which moments those are depends on where you stand.

Dart's `DateTime` knows this about itself, which is why it carries a flag:

<include cwd lang="dart" meta='title="test/instant_test.dart"'>../code/ch30_expenses/test/instant_test.dart#equality</include>

The first of the four, and the only one you have already seen: 25.4 measured it to justify
`Day`, and the assertions have moved here so that all four live in one file. Read the second
and third tests together. `local` and `utc` are **the same moment** — the same microsecond
count, and `isAtSameMomentAs` says so — and they are **not equal**, because `==` compares
`isUtc` as well as the instant. And they have **the same `hashCode`**, because that is derived
from the instant alone.

Study 25 put that pair in bold: equal hash codes are not evidence of equality. It is a rule you
rarely get to see broken, and here it is in `dart:core`, on a type every program uses. It is
not a bug. Two readings of the same moment, one in local time and one in UTC, genuinely are
different readings — which is `==` being a question each type answers for itself, and
`DateTime` answering it in a way almost nobody expects.

<Gloss title="So which comparison did you want?">
`isAtSameMomentAs` is instant equality and ignores the flag. `isBefore` and `isAfter` are the
same: they compare moments, not calendars.

`==` is the one to be careful with, because it is the one you get by accident — from
`expect`, from `contains`, from a `Set`, from a `Map` key. A `Set<DateTime>` holding the same
instant twice, once local and once UTC, has two elements in it. That is worth knowing before
you meet it.
</Gloss>

And it does not write down which reading it was:

<include cwd lang="dart" meta='title="test/instant_test.dart"'>../code/ch30_expenses/test/instant_test.dart#offset</include>

`2026-09-09T00:00:00.000`. No `Z`, no `+05:00`, nothing at all to say which midnight that was
— and the string is byte-identical whether the program ran in Tashkent, London or New York,
where it named three different moments. Study 29 said this in one paragraph to justify storing
a `Day`; here it is with the rest of its consequences attached.

The fourth test is the one worth staring at. A timestamp written by somebody five hours ahead
of Greenwich, on the **10th**, parses to a `DateTime` whose `day` is **9**. `DateTime.parse`
converts to UTC and keeps the instant, which is the right thing to do with an instant and
throws away the calendar day the writer meant. Two strings naming different days are `==`,
because as instants they are the same moment.

## 30.2 What a DateTime will not refuse

The second trap is the one the Drill opens with, and it is quieter:

<include cwd lang="dart" meta='title="test/instant_test.dart"'>../code/ch30_expenses/test/instant_test.dart#overflow</include>

There is no such date as the 31st of February, and `DateTime` builds one anyway by rolling
forward into March. The 13th month is next January. Month and day arithmetic overflows in
silence and **never throws**, which means there is no error to catch and nothing to test for
— the only way to find out is to compare against the answer you expected.

The third test is why this belongs in a study about a file format. Study 29 stores
`2026-09-09` and reads it back with `Day.parse`, which answers `null` for anything that is not
a real day, and study 29's tests prove it. Had it stored a `DateTime` string instead, a file
somebody edited badly would not be rejected and would not throw. It would come back as a
different day, and the program would carry on.

Then the last test, which is the honest half. Day zero of a month is the last day of the
month before — `DateTime(2026, 3, 0)` really is the 28th of February, and `DateTime(2024, 3, 0)`
really is the 29th. It is the standard trick for asking how long a month is, it is correct,
and it works by *exactly the mechanism that produced the bug three tests above it*.

This book writes the table out instead:

```dart title="lib/src/day.dart"
static int lastDayOf(int year, int month) => switch (month) { … }
```

Study 25 shipped that method with an underscore in front of it, and this study took the
underscore off. That is the whole change. `Period` needs to know how long a month is,
`Period` lives in another file, and study 23's rule is that a file is the privacy boundary
— so a helper that two types share cannot stay private to one of them.

## 30.3 A month is not a length of time

The third trap is the one people reach for after they have learned the first two:

<include cwd lang="dart" meta='title="test/instant_test.dart"'>../code/ch30_expenses/test/instant_test.dart#duration</include>

A `Duration` is a count of microseconds. `Duration(days: 1)` is not *a day*, it is twenty-four
hours, and `inDays` divides and truncates rather than consulting a calendar. There is no
`Duration` that means *a month*, because February 2026 is 28 days, March is 31 and February
2024 is 29, and no arithmetic on microseconds turns one of those into another.

So a period is not a start and a length:

<include cwd lang="dart" meta='title="lib/src/period.dart"'>../code/ch30_expenses/lib/src/period.dart#period</include>

Two integers, both of them things a person says out loud, and nothing in the type is an
instant — so there is nothing in it with a time zone to get wrong. `first` is always the 1st.
`last` is the only interesting one, and it is where `Day.lastDayOf` earns being public.

`contains` is two integer comparisons and deliberately no ordering. A calendar month *is* the
days that share its year and its month. Asking whether a day sits between `first` and `last`
would be a longer road to the same answer with two ends to get wrong, and it would need `Day`
to be comparable, which it is not yet — study 31 has a reason to want that and this study does
not.

It is a value by study 25's rules, which by now is a checklist rather than an argument: equal
contents mean equal periods, `hashCode` agrees, and a month that has never existed cannot be
built.

Study 28 called `async` contagious and it is, but it is worth being precise about how it
spreads: **along calls**. A function that awaits is a function whose callers await. `totals`
awaited because it was reached through something that waits, not because adding money up
takes time — and moving it off the interface removed the call, which removed the future.
That is the shape of the cure, and it is the only one there is: `async` does not come off a
function, it comes off the path to it.

The range line is not decoration. `list 2026-02` says `2026-02-01 to 2026-02-28`, and that is
the program telling you what it actually covered rather than what you asked for. In a leap
year it says the 29th. It is one line of output and it is the only place a reader can check

<Gloss title="Daylight saving, which this study will not fake">
Every trap above reproduces on your machine. Daylight saving does not, and that is precisely
why it is dangerous.

Twice a year, in most of the world, a local day is 23 or 25 hours long — and once a year one
local time happens twice. `DateTime` handles the instants correctly and cannot help you with
the calendar: adding `Duration(hours: 24)` to a local `DateTime` lands on a different
wall-clock time than it started at, and dart.dev's own warning says so.

A transcript of that would depend on the reader's machine having the right timezone
database and the right timezone, and this book does not print output it cannot promise you
will see. So: the hazard is named, and the answer is
[`package:timezone`](https://pub.dev/packages/timezone) — published by `labs.dart.dev`, which
ships the IANA database and a `TZDateTime` that knows which rules were in force at a given
moment. Nothing in this program needs it, because nothing in this program stores an instant.
</Gloss>

<Practice>
**Do not store an instant where a date belongs.** This is the book's own convention and
carries no citation, because no published Dart guideline says it — the closest is a warning
rather than a rule, quoted below.

A `DateTime` answers *which moment*. A birthday, an invoice date, a public holiday and the
day an expense happened all answer *which box on the calendar*, which is a different question
with a different answer in every time zone at once. Putting one in the other's place is not a
precision problem that a well-chosen format string will fix; the value is wrong before it is
ever printed.

The rule of thumb that follows: **let instants live at the edges.** Read one where the outside
world hands you one — `bin/expenses.dart` calls `DateTime.now()` and is the only file in this
package that does — convert it immediately, and let everything inside work in the type that
matches the question. Study 27 made the conversion a parameter, so nothing below `main` has
even that much contact with a clock.

The published caution that comes nearest is on dart.dev's
[dart:core tour](https://dart.dev/libraries/dart-core#dates-and-times): *"Using a `Duration`
to shift a `DateTime` by days can be problematic, due to clock shifts (to daylight saving
time, for example). Use UTC dates if you must shift days."* That is one consequence of the
rule above rather than the rule itself, and it is worth reading as the tip of it.
</Practice>

## Wrapping up

A `DateTime` is an instant, and four separate things follow from that, every one of them
measured above rather than remembered. Study 25 needed the first to justify `Day`; the other
three are what this study came for.

It is not equal to the same moment in another zone, though it hashes the same — which is
study 25's rule about hash codes, broken in the standard library, on purpose.

It writes itself down with no offset, and reading a string that *has* an offset throws the
offset away along with the calendar day it was written with.

It refuses nothing. The 31st of February is the 3rd of March, silently and without an error to
catch, and `DateTime.parse` does it too, which is how a badly edited file becomes a wrong
answer rather than a rejected one.

And a `Duration` is microseconds, so no `Duration` means *a month*. `Period` is a year and a
month for that reason, and works out its own days from the calendar.

The rule underneath all four: let instants live at the edges. One call to `DateTime.now()`, in
the one file no test runs, converted immediately into the type that matches the question.

Also met: `isAtSameMomentAs`, `isUtc`, `toUtc`, `DateTime.parse` and its silence, `Duration`
and `inDays`, a second value type built to study 25's rules, and an extension that moved from
an interface to the data — taking a `Future` with it.

Study 31 turns `list <month>` into a report worth the name: ordering, which needs `Comparable`,
and adding money up, which needs `Money` to have an `operator +`.
